Most organisations in Egypt and the Gulf are now on their second or third conversation about AI
training. The first one usually produced an impressive day and very little change. This is a guide
to making the next one produce something.

There are nine questions worth asking. Price is not the first of them.

## 1. Who will actually stand in the room?

The single most common disappointment in corporate training in this region is buying a name and
receiving an associate.

Ask directly: who delivers the session? What have they run outside a training room? An instructor
who has held budget responsibility argues differently from one who has only ever taught — because
they have had to defend a decision that cost money.

## 2. What language will it genuinely be delivered in?

"We can deliver in Arabic" covers three very different realities: a trainer who thinks in Arabic; a
trainer working through an interpreter; and English slides read aloud with occasional Arabic
asides.

For most teams in Egypt and the Gulf, the format that works is verbal delivery in Arabic with
English slides and materials. Participants ask real questions in the language they think in, while
the written material stays in the language of their systems.

Ask which of the three you are being sold.

## 3. What proportion is hands-on — and what will people actually produce?

Ask for the agenda and count. If more than a third is presentation, the team will enjoy it and
change nothing.

The sharper version of the question: **what will each participant have produced by the end of the
day?** A good answer is specific — three rebuilt workflows, a redesigned assessment, a one-page AI
position. A vague answer means slides.

## 4. Will exercises run on our own data?

Generic exercises are safe, easy to prepare, and forgettable. Working on the organisation's real
documents is the difference between a team that understands the idea and a team that has already
done the work once.

This has a prerequisite, which is question five.

## 5. How will the data boundary be handled?

If a provider proposes running exercises on your real material without first establishing what may
and may not be entered into a public AI tool, that is disqualifying.

In regulated sectors — energy, financial services, government-related entities — the boundary
session should come first, before any exercise touches real material. Done properly it does not
limit the training. It is what makes using real material possible at all.

## 6. Does it teach tools, or judgement?

Tool training has a shelf life of about a quarter. Interfaces change, features move, and the
provider's carefully prepared screenshots go stale.

What does not go stale is the ability to brief a model properly, to ground it in a real source, and
to tell when the output is wrong. Ask what the participant will still be able to do in eighteen
months.

## 7. Is the seniority right?

An AI session for a leadership team is not a shortened version of the team session. Executives need
to make decisions about funding, sequencing, risk and accountability. Their teams need to produce
work.

Mixing the two in one room usually serves neither. Ask whether the provider distinguishes them —
and if the same deck appears for both, that is your answer.

## 8. What happens in the thirty days afterwards?

The failure mode is well documented: enthusiasm for two weeks, then reversion.

Ask what mechanism carries the learning forward. A written adoption plan per participant, a shared
prompt library the team keeps, a named owner for each workflow, a review point in the calendar.
"We'll send the slides" is not a mechanism.

## 9. What is the evidence?

Named clients, participant numbers, ratings. Not "trained hundreds of professionals" — which client,
how many people, what did they say.

Where a provider can point to repeat engagements, that is stronger evidence than any satisfaction
score. A first round can be sold on enthusiasm. A second has to be earned.

## The order matters more than the provider

One structural point, independent of who you choose.

Start with the leadership group, not with a departmental pilot. Departmental training without
leadership involvement produces pockets of enthusiasm that fade, because nobody in the room controls
priorities or budget.

When the leadership team has a shared position first, departmental training lands into an
organisation that has already decided what it is trying to achieve. That sequence — leadership, then
departments, with the data boundary set at the front — is what produced 65 trained executives and a
4.8 out of 5 rating at Kahraba, the National Electricity Technology Company.
