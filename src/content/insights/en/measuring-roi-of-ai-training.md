The return on corporate AI training can be measured. Most of the numbers you will be shown cannot be
trusted, because they are produced after the fact by the party being paid.

Honest measurement needs four things decided **before** anyone books a date: which recurring tasks
you are watching, how long they take now, who owns the measurement, and when you will look again.
Nothing after that is complicated. Nothing before it can be recovered later.

## What can honestly be measured

**Time on named recurring tasks.** Pick two or three things the team does every week — a weekly
report, a tender summary, a first-draft proposal, a budget variance commentary. Record how long each
takes today and who does it. These are measurable because they are specific, repeated, and owned by
someone who can tell you the truth about them.

**Throughput on one process.** Not "marketing productivity". One process: campaign briefs produced
per month, support tickets resolved without escalation, proposals out of the door. One number, one
owner, one definition that does not move.

**Sustained use at sixty days.** The single most informative measurement in corporate training, and
almost nobody takes it. Ask every participant one question two months later: *did you use any of
this in your actual work last week?* The proportion who say yes is the real result of the programme.
Enthusiasm at the end of a workshop is free; use at sixty days is not.

**Quality against a standard you already have.** If you have a review step — a manager who checks
proposals, a compliance read, an editor — ask whether the rework rate moved. You already have the
instrument. You do not need a new one.

## What cannot honestly be measured

**A company-wide productivity percentage.** There is no clean way to isolate the effect of two days
of training from hiring, seasonality, a reorganisation, a new system or a good quarter. Any provider
quoting you one is estimating, and the estimate was built to be quoted.

**Self-reported hours saved.** People overestimate time saved on tasks they now enjoy more.
Collected after a good workshop, these numbers are a mood reading.

**Satisfaction scores.** They measure whether the day was well run. That is worth knowing — it is
simply not the same thing. A programme can average 4.8 out of 5 and change nothing, because
participants rate the experience before anything has been asked of them. A high score earns you the
right to ask for the sixty-day number. It is not a substitute for it.

**Completion rates.** Completion measures attendance.

## Set it up before you book

Four decisions, half an hour, before the agenda is written:

| Decision | Example |
|---|---|
| Which tasks are we watching? | The weekly commercial report and the first draft of client proposals |
| What is the baseline? | 6 hours and 4 hours per week respectively, done by two named people |
| Who owns the measurement? | The department head — not the trainer |
| When do we look? | 30 days, 60 days (adoption question), 90 days |

Hand those tasks to the provider as the design brief. A programme built around the two things you
have agreed to measure is a different programme from one built around a generic agenda — and if the
provider cannot build around them, you have learned something useful before spending anything.

## The arithmetic nobody does

Before comparing fees, price the room. Twenty managers away from their work for two days is a large
internal cost in salary alone, and you incur it whether or not the training works. Against that, the
difference between two providers' fees is usually small.

This cuts both ways. It is an argument against haggling over the fee, and it is an argument for
being ruthless about whether anything will change — because the expensive part of bad training is
never the invoice.

## "Why not just buy online course seats?"

A fair question, and for part of the problem the answer is yes. A self-paced library is the right
tool for broad individual upskilling, and it costs a fraction as much per head.

It is the wrong tool for three things. It contains nothing about your processes, your constraints or
your data. It asks for no decisions, so no decisions get made. And participation is optional, which
in practice means it is partial. If the objective is "our managers should know what these tools do",
buy the library. If the objective is "our management team should agree what we are going to change",
that requires facilitated time, and no amount of video substitutes for it.

Most organisations need both and buy only one.

## What a good provider will tell you

That the effect is not uniform. Some departments will change how they work within a fortnight;
others will not move at all, usually because their work is less document-heavy or because their head
of function was not in the room. A provider who promises an even gain across the organisation is
selling you a number, not a programme.

The two measurements worth insisting on — baseline task times, and the sixty-day adoption
question — cost nothing and settle the argument either way. Ask for them in writing, and keep the
measuring yourself.
