Kahraba is Egypt's largest private-sector power generation and distribution company. When the
engagement began, I expected the hard part to be the technology. It was not.

Sixty-five executives later — across three runs of the same 16-hour workshop, rated 4.8 out of 5 —
these are the five things I would tell anyone planning corporate AI training in a serious
organisation.

## 1. Run it more than once

The conventional advice — mine included — is to start at the top. Kahraba happened the other way
round, and it taught me something I did not expect.

We ran the same 16-hour workshop three times, 20 to 23 executives per run. The first run is where
you discover which exercises actually land. By the third, the weak sections had been cut and the
strong ones extended. A format delivered once is a hypothesis; delivered three times to comparable
groups, it is evidence.

And the leadership session came *after* — the CEO and nine senior managers booking eight hours on
delegated AI work once the organisation had already proved it could use the tools. That order has a
quiet advantage: the leadership conversation was about extending something real, not authorising
something speculative. I would still usually argue for leadership first. But a pilot that earns the
leadership session is a legitimate second route, and this engagement is why I now say so.

## 2. The data boundary comes before the first exercise, not after it

In a power company, a large share of what an executive handles daily cannot go into a public AI tool
under any circumstances. My instinct beforehand was that this would constrain the training.

The opposite turned out to be true — provided the boundary conversation came *first*.

We spent the opening session agreeing explicitly what could and could not leave the organisation.
Once that was settled and written down, the room could work on genuinely real material for the rest
of the programme. Had we left the boundary to the end, every exercise would have run on sanitised
examples, and sanitised examples are why most corporate training is forgotten by Friday.

The boundary is not a restriction on the training. It is the precondition for the training being
real.

## 3. Seniority changes the question, not just the pace

Early on I assumed the executive session would be a compressed version of the team session. That was
wrong, and I changed it.

Senior people are not primarily asking *how do I use this*. They are asking:

- Which of these investments is real and which is theatre?
- What do I demand from the people who report to me?
- Where does accountability sit when an AI-assisted decision turns out badly?
- What do I stop funding?

Those are not advanced versions of the practitioner questions. They are different questions, and
they need different exercises — which is why Menova now runs AI for Executives and AI for Business
as genuinely separate programmes rather than long and short versions of one.

## 4. Scepticism in the room is an asset

Engineers in a power company are professionally sceptical, and they should be. Several participants
arrived expecting to be sold something.

The most productive move was to lead with the failure modes — to demonstrate, early and
deliberately, a confident wrong answer, and to explain why it happened. Establishing that the
instructor will name the limitations bought more credibility than any capability demonstration
could have.

If a room is not sceptical, that is the harder audience. Uncritical enthusiasm produces the
unverified output that damages an organisation later.

## 5. What people rate is what they built

A 4.8 out of 5 was not awarded for the content. It was awarded because participants spent most of
the programme rebuilding their own recurring work, on their own documents, and left holding a
written plan for their own area.

That is the whole design principle: 70% hands-on, and a demonstration every ninety minutes that
genuinely surprises the room — a task that normally takes an afternoon, completed and verified in
four minutes, on the organisation's own data.

Nobody rates a lecture 4.8. They rate the afternoon they got back.

## The pattern, generalised

Run it more than once. Boundary before exercises. Separate programmes for separate seniorities. Lead
with the failure modes. Make people build.

None of that is specific to energy. It is what I would now recommend to any organisation of any size
that wants AI training to survive contact with the following Monday.
